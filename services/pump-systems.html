Signs Your Well Pump Needs Attention

Well pumps are out of sight and out of mind until something goes wrong. Here are the warning signs that your pump system needs professional attention:

  • No water. The most obvious sign. If you turn on the tap and nothing comes out, the pump may have failed, the pressure tank may be waterlogged, or there could be an electrical issue.
  • Low water pressure. A gradual decline in water pressure can indicate a pump that is losing capacity, a pressure tank issue, or a leak in the supply line between the well and the house.
  • Pump cycling on and off frequently. This is called short cycling and usually points to a failing pressure tank. When the tank cannot hold pressure, the pump has to turn on every time water is used. This dramatically increases wear on the pump motor.
  • Sputtering or air in the water. Air bursts from the faucet can mean the pump is drawing air, which could indicate a drop in the water table, a crack in the drop pipe, or a failing pump.
  • Higher electric bills. A pump that is working harder than it should, whether from a failing motor, a waterlogged pressure tank, or a leak, will use more electricity.
  • Dirty or sandy water. If sediment or sand suddenly appears in your water, the pump may be drawing from near the bottom of the well, or the well screen may be damaged.

Understanding Your Well Pump System

If you live on a rural property in Lincoln County with a private well, your water system has several key components that work together:

  • The well pump. This is the motor and pump assembly that lifts water from the well to the surface. Most residential wells in our area use submersible pumps that sit inside the well casing below the water line. Shallower wells may use jet pumps located at the surface.
  • The pressure tank. This tank stores water under pressure and delivers it to the house when you turn on a faucet. It prevents the pump from running constantly by maintaining a reserve of pressurized water. The pressure switch on the tank tells the pump when to turn on and off.
  • The supply line. This is the pipe that runs from the well to the house. In Lincoln County's climate, supply lines should be buried below the frost line and properly insulated where they enter the building.
  • The electrical system. The pump requires a dedicated electrical circuit, typically with a control box for submersible pumps. Wiring problems, a tripped breaker, or a failed control box can shut down the pump.

When any one of these components fails, you lose water. The good news is that most pump system problems are diagnosable and repairable. The key is knowing which component is actually causing the issue rather than replacing parts until something works.

When to Replace Your Well Pump

Well pumps do not last forever. Here are the situations where replacement makes more sense than repair:

  • Age. If your submersible pump is over 10 to 15 years old and showing symptoms, replacement is usually the better investment. The motor and bearings wear out over time, and repair costs can approach the price of a new pump.
  • Repeated failures. If you have had the pump repaired more than once in the past couple of years, the system is declining and further repairs will be short-lived.
  • Motor failure. A burned-out or seized pump motor in a submersible pump is not economical to repair. The pump needs to be pulled from the well and replaced.
  • Insufficient capacity. If your household water demand has increased, whether from a home addition, new bathroom, or other changes, the existing pump may not have the capacity to keep up. Upsizing the pump is a replacement, not a repair.

Rural Water Systems on the Oregon Coast

Living on a rural property in Lincoln County means your water system is your responsibility. Unlike properties on municipal water, there is no city utility to call when the water stops. Understanding the basics of your system and knowing who to call when something goes wrong is important.

The Oregon Coast environment presents specific challenges for well water systems. The wet climate keeps the water table relatively high, which is good for well productivity. However, the high moisture also means more corrosion on exposed metal components, and the dense vegetation means root intrusion is always a possibility for supply lines that run near trees.
